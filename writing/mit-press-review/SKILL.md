# MIT Press Review

Apply MIT Press's public editorial criteria to author-facing review and revision. Improve how worthwhile ideas reach their intended readers while preserving the author's meaning, expertise, and voice.

This skill is a practical synthesis, not an official MIT Press rubric, acceptance assessment, or commissioned peer review. The public sources reviewed do not provide a universal numerical scoring system.

## Establish the Context

Infer the purpose, intended reader, assumed knowledge, form, and requested deliverable from the material and conversation. State consequential assumptions briefly; ask only when a missing choice changes the work materially.

- For a review, return findings and representative edits without changing the source file.
- For an edit or rewrite, produce the requested revision and identify unresolved substantive questions.
- For a proposal, examine the reader benefit, distinctive contribution, proposed coverage, and evidence that the author can deliver the book.
- For an excerpt, assess what is visible. Do not make whole-book claims about organization, originality, or consistency.

Use the core criteria below for ordinary writing work. Read [references/source-notes.md](references/source-notes.md) when attributing a rule, applying book-type conventions, checking submission readiness, or resolving the distinction between author revision and formal peer review. It records source links, scope, and research date.

Respect supplied book-specific editorial decisions. Verify current official guidance before asserting submission compliance; if verification is unavailable, identify the dated basis and limit the conclusion. Apply a journal's own guidance to journal submissions instead of transferring book rules.

## Review the Writing

Start with purpose and organization, then inspect paragraphs and sentences. The first column identifies the public criterion; the diagnostic questions are this skill's editorial interpretation, not a published MIT Press checklist.

| Criterion and source basis | Editorial diagnosis |
| --- | --- |
| **Reader value** — proposal guidance asks why the work matters to its readers. | Identify the reader's likely question and the draft's promised answer. Flag an opening that announces a topic without establishing what readers will gain. |
| **Audience fit** — proposal guidance asks authors to specify readership, level, and prerequisites. | Track unstated prerequisites, undefined terms, unexplained examples, and unnecessary background. Keep precise specialist language when the intended reader needs or knows it. |
| **Structure and exposition** — reviewers receive a detailed contents list and samples demonstrating the appeal of the explanatory approach. | Trace the argument or teaching sequence. Find missing conceptual steps, misplaced background, repetition, and examples that do not explain the point. Recommend moves or additions before polishing passages that need restructuring. |
| **Clarity and precision** — explicitly requested in sample prose. | Check ambiguous references, abstract noun clusters, unstable terminology, unclear actors, and changes in claim strength. Offer a specific revision; query passages with multiple plausible meanings. |
| **Originality and contribution** — explicitly evaluated in peer review. | Distinguish the contribution the author claims from what the text demonstrates. Check whether competing work is represented fairly. A new synthesis or explanatory approach can be valuable; do not demand new empirical research from every form. External novelty requires literature assessment. |
| **Scholarly responsibility** — author guidance requires responsible use of others' work and dependable documentation. | Check whether claims have identifiable support, quotations retain their meaning, and evidence is distinguished from interpretation. Flag unverified claims for checking; a citation's presence does not establish that it supports the sentence. |
| **Accessible depth** — especially explicit for Essential Knowledge books. | Check whether readers can build a useful understanding from the fundamentals. Preserve necessary distinctions and qualifications when shortening. Use examples or definitions when they solve a specific comprehension problem. |

Preserve effective passages as well as identifying weaknesses. Treat voice, narrative detail, sentence variety, and technical depth as resources to assess in context. Do not impose blanket bans on passive voice, first person, jargon, long sentences, or repetition. Do not substitute readability scores or fixed sentence lengths for reader-specific judgment.

## Apply Editing and Preparation Rules Selectively

For a prose review, keep production details out of the findings unless they affect comprehension or are requested. For manuscript preparation, use the applicable source-notes section to check citation consistency, heading hierarchy, reader aids, and author review responsibilities.

The general author guidance prefers Chicago style and emphasizes consistent application. Do not invent Chicago rules from memory or present a permitted alternative as a violation. Distinguish a verified source mismatch from an incomplete citation and from a stylistic preference.

When revising:

- Solve the identified reader problem with the smallest effective change.
- Preserve facts, uncertainty, technical conditions, quotations, and the author's stance. Do not strengthen a conclusion to make it sound decisive.
- Leave substantive gaps visible through a query or clearly marked proposed addition. Do not invent evidence, citations, examples presented as factual, or author intentions.
- Compare the revision with the original for lost qualifications, definitions, logical steps, and voice.

## Report Actionable Findings

Lead with the draft's apparent purpose and the changes with the greatest reader impact. Scale the output to the requested scope; a paragraph edit does not need a manuscript audit report.

For each finding, provide a location or short excerpt, the reader problem, a proposed edit or action, and its basis. Keep the basis separate from the impact:

- **Published requirement:** a directly applicable preparation rule; link the source and identify the book type or stage.
- **Published criterion:** an explicit evaluation dimension such as clarity or contribution; explain the observed weakness without presenting one rewrite as mandatory.
- **Editorial recommendation:** this skill's judgment about structure, explanation, or prose.
- **Query:** evidence, intended meaning, or a book-specific decision remains unresolved.

Group repeated problems with representative examples. Include strengths worth preserving and briefly identify any material limits, such as unreviewed chapters or sources that were not checked. Do not claim factual verification, exhaustive coverage, publisher approval, or acceptance prospects beyond the work performed. Use a numerical score only if requested, and label it as a locally defined assessment.
