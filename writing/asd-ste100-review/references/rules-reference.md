# ASD-STE100 Issue 9 Rules Reference

This is an operational paraphrase of Part 1 of ASD-STE100 Issue 9 (2025-01-15). It is not a replacement for the official standard or its dictionary. Use the official publication to resolve questions and to verify approved words.

## Section 1: Words

### Rule 1.1 — Select from the permitted vocabulary classes

Use a word only when it is an approved dictionary word, an eligible technical noun, or an eligible technical verb. The Part 2 dictionary does not contain the domain-specific term inventory; verify those terms in the applicable company glossary or terminology database.

### Rule 1.2 — Keep the approved part of speech

Use a dictionary word only as the part of speech assigned to that entry. A familiar English use is not permitted merely because the spelling is identical. When an approved alternative has a different part of speech, reconstruct the sentence instead of performing a mechanical substitution.

### Rule 1.3 — Keep the approved meaning

Use an approved word only with the meaning stated in the dictionary entry. Treat a restricted STE meaning as controlling even when standard English gives the word other meanings.

### Rule 1.4 — Use only listed verb and adjective forms

For a verb, use only forms given in its entry. For an adjective, use the base form and only the comparative or superlative forms permitted by the entry; adjectives normally formed with *more* and *most* need not list those two constructions.

### Rule 1.5 — Admit technical nouns by category

A technical noun must denote a defined concept in a subject field and fit at least one of the 22 categories in [standard-and-dictionary.md](standard-and-dictionary.md). The examples in the official standard illustrate the categories; they are not closed lists.

### Rule 1.6 — Restrict unapproved words used as technical nouns

A word that the dictionary rejects, or does not list, can appear only in a context where it is an eligible technical noun or part of one. Permission is contextual: the same spelling may be disallowed in an ordinary meaning but allowed in a recognized domain term.

### Rule 1.7 — Do not turn a technical noun into a verb

Keep a technical noun in noun function, including as a noun modifier in another technical noun. If the action has no approved verb, reconstruct it with an approved verb and the technical noun. The same spelling may act as a technical verb only when it independently satisfies a Rule 1.12 category in that context.

### Rule 1.8 — Use authorized domain terminology

Prefer the technical noun approved by the company, industry, project, or subject field. Verify official parts information, drawings, documentation, glossaries, and terminology databases; do not replace an official name with a plausible synonym.

### Rule 1.9 — Make a newly selected technical noun short and clear

When no authorized term exists, select a term that is easy to understand and normally no longer than three words. Use identifiers and illustrations to avoid unnecessary modifiers. Add modifiers only when the reader needs them to distinguish the item.

### Rule 1.10 — Exclude regional language, slang, and jargon

Do not select a technical noun understood only in a limited geographic or professional group. Use a broadly recognized technical term that preserves the specified meaning.

### Rule 1.11 — Use one technical noun for one item

Do not alternate among names for the same item. Apply the authorized term consistently across steps, paragraphs, tables, figures, and cross-references unless an official short form has been defined.

### Rule 1.12 — Admit technical verbs by category and context

A technical verb must denote a defined process in a subject field and fit a category in [standard-and-dictionary.md](standard-and-dictionary.md). Prefer an approved dictionary verb when it expresses the action accurately. Use a technical verb only in its qualifying context, choose the precise process rather than a vague action, and obey all Section 3 verb rules.

### Rule 1.13 — Do not turn a technical verb into a noun

Keep a technical verb in verb function. Its approved past participle can modify a technical noun when it describes a condition. The same spelling may act as a technical noun only when it independently satisfies Rule 1.5 in that context.

### Rule 1.14 — Default to American English spelling

Use the spelling in the STE dictionary unless a governing publication specification, style guide, contract, or other official directive requires a different variety. Preserve British or other spelling inside immutable quoted text.

## Section 2: Multi-word Nouns

### Rule 2.1 — Limit ordinary multi-word nouns to three words

Use no more than three words in a multi-word noun. Break a long modifier stack with prepositions, relative clauses, or direct actions so the relationship to the head noun is clear. Apply Rule 2.2 to an authorized technical noun that cannot be changed.

### Rule 2.2 — Introduce and clarify authorized long technical nouns

Write an official technical noun of more than three words in full on first use. Then either define an authorized shorter form or abbreviation, or hyphenate words that genuinely operate as one unit.

- Do not abbreviate short terms merely to reduce length, and avoid abbreviation-heavy procedures.
- Do not create a misleading hyphen grouping or join an entire long string with hyphens.
- Preserve hyphens already present in an official technical noun.
- Do not add hyphens to a clear term of three words or fewer without another reason.
- If grouping is uncertain, explain the full term and use an approved short form or abbreviation.

## Section 3: Verbs

### Rule 3.1 — Use only dictionary-listed forms

Check the complete verb entry. Do not generate a regular form by analogy when that form is absent.

### Rule 3.2 — Restrict forms and tenses

Permitted forms are the infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future with *will*, and the past participle used as an adjective. Do not use perfect or progressive tenses or other complex verb constructions.

### Rule 3.3 — Use the past participle as an adjective only

An approved past participle can describe an item's condition before a noun or after a form of *be*, *become*, or *stay*. This adjectival use is not passive voice. Some dictionary adjectives resemble participles of verbs that are not approved; follow the assigned adjective entry.

### Rule 3.4 — Avoid auxiliary-based complex constructions

Do not combine *have* with a past participle to create a perfect tense. Avoid auxiliary-plus-participle constructions that create complex passive forms. Convert a procedure to an imperative or put the known agent in subject position.

### Rule 3.5 — Confine “-ing” forms to technical nouns

Use an “-ing” form only as a technical noun or as a functional modifier within a technical noun. Do not use it to create progressive verbs, gerund clauses, general adjectives, reduced clauses, or long modifier strings. A small number of “-ing” words are independently approved as nouns, adjectives, a pronoun, or a preposition; verify each in the dictionary.

### Rule 3.6 — Prefer active voice; permit a narrow descriptive exception

Use active voice in procedures and normally in descriptions. Passive voice is permitted only in descriptive writing when the agent is genuinely unknown. Do not invent an incorrect agent to force active voice; *something* can serve when an unspecified agent is accurate.

To repair passive voice:

- Move a stated agent into subject position.
- Promote the meaningful action from an infinitive construction.
- In a procedure, use the imperative.
- In a description, use *you* for the reader or *we* for the issuing organization when accurate.

### Rule 3.7 — Express an action with an approved verb

Prefer a direct approved verb over an action noun or other indirect construction. If the desired spelling is not approved as a verb, reconstruct the sentence with its approved part of speech rather than converting it.

## Section 4: Sentences

### Rule 4.1 — Make sentences short, clear, concrete, and accurate

Apply the type-specific limits in Sections 5 and 6. Give a procedure directly to the reader. In a description, keep one topic in each sentence and develop it progressively. Replace abstract assertions with observable actions, conditions, quantities, and results; do not add technical detail that the source does not support.

### Rule 4.2 — Do not shorten by omission or contraction

Keep the necessary subject, verb, noun, article, and other grammatical parts. Spell contractions out. Expand telegraphic fragments and ambiguous coordination even when the result contains more words.

### Rule 4.3 — Use vertical lists for complex material

Use a vertical list when many items or actions make prose difficult to parse.

- End the lead-in with a colon.
- Give each item an identifier such as a dash, bullet, letter, or number as the external publication rules permit.
- Start each item with an uppercase letter and use an article where grammar requires it.
- End a complete-sentence item with a period; do not end a fragment item with a period.
- Do not put a comma or semicolon at an item's end; put a period after the final item.
- Make every item connect grammatically and logically to the lead-in.
- Do not mix procedural and descriptive writing in one list.
- Repeat a necessary negative command in each applicable safety-list item.
- Avoid confusing nested levels; keep peer items at the same level.
- Apply Rule 8.4 separately to the lead-in and each item.

The marker shape, indentation, and other typography come from the applicable publication specification, not STE.

### Rule 4.4 — Connect related sentences explicitly

Use approved connecting words or phrases to show addition, contrast, sequence, or result. Repeated key terms and clear demonstrative adjectives can also connect ideas. In procedures, use a connector when an explanation after a work step needs it; use one in a safety instruction when it makes the relationship between prevention and result clear. Verify that each connector states the real logical relationship.

### Rule 4.5 — Supply articles or demonstrative adjectives where applicable

Use *a*, *an*, *the*, *this*, or *these* before a noun or multi-word noun when English grammar requires one. Do not add an article to a general plural, mass noun, abstract concept, or other construction that does not take it.

- In a short series, articles can make each item unambiguous.
- In a long series, one article before the first item can improve readability, but check whether a shared adjective applies to all items or only one.
- Do not put a definite article before a noun immediately identified by an alphanumeric designator when the identifier makes the combination a proper name.

## Section 5: Procedural Writing

### Rule 5.1 — Keep procedural sentences to 20 words

Count no more than 20 words in each instruction, warning, caution, or other safety sentence. A note is descriptive and can contain up to 25 words. Apply the special counting rules in Section 8 rather than a whitespace count.

### Rule 5.2 — Put one instruction in each sentence

Separate sequential or independent actions into distinct work steps. Two or more instructions can share a sentence only when the actions occur at the same time. A work step may contain multiple sentences when actions are simultaneous or when a result or limit follows the action immediately and belongs to it.

### Rule 5.3 — Use the imperative for instructions

Start a direct instruction with the command form. Do not hide a required action in passive, descriptive, or future wording. Do not add *must* before an imperative unless the word is necessary for an important safety instruction or an important condition.

### Rule 5.4 — Put a prerequisite condition before the command

When the reader needs a condition first, place the descriptive condition at the start, add a comma, and then give the imperative instruction. Recheck comma placement because it can change which verb an adverb modifies.

### Rule 5.5 — Keep notes informational

A note can give helpful descriptive information but must not contain an instruction, requirement, limit, tolerance, work-step result, or safety precaution.

- Do not use the imperative in a note.
- Keep each note sentence to 25 words.
- Put a required limit or result immediately after its action in the work step.
- Convert harm-prevention information to the correct warning or caution.
- Test the procedure without its notes; the reader must still be able to complete it correctly and safely.
- In a description, use a note only when necessary for an illustration or table that belongs to that description.

## Section 6: Descriptive Writing

Descriptions include system or component information, general technical information, and notes in procedures. They give information, not commands; do not use the imperative.

### Rule 6.1 — Introduce information progressively

Begin with the governing topic and add one subject at a time in reader order. Do not overload an early sentence with details whose context has not yet been established.

### Rule 6.2 — Build logic with stable key words and phrases

Repeat the important noun or phrase that connects a new sentence to the prior one. Use approved connectors where they clarify the relationship. Do not vary terminology for style when repetition is what maintains the logic.

### Rule 6.3 — Keep descriptive sentences to 25 words

Count no more than 25 words in each descriptive sentence, including each sentence in a note. Apply every special counting rule in Section 8.

### Rule 6.4 — Group related information in paragraphs

Use a topic sentence at the start of a paragraph, then develop that topic in logical sequence. Start a new paragraph when the topic or information function changes.

### Rule 6.5 — Keep one topic in each paragraph

Make the first sentence identify and connect the paragraph's topic. As an outline test, read only the topic sentences; they should accurately show the document's structure.

### Rule 6.6 — Limit a paragraph to six sentences

Use no more than six sentences in a paragraph. Split a longer paragraph into coherent topic units instead of making an arbitrary break.

## Section 7: Safety Instructions

In the aerospace and defense context, a warning addresses injury or death; a caution addresses damage to objects. Another domain can use different labels or symbols, but the three content rules still apply. Perform or rely on an authorized risk analysis; language review cannot determine the actual risk by itself.

### Rule 7.1 — Identify the risk level correctly

Use the applicable signal word or symbol so the reader immediately knows the level of risk. Use a warning when injury or death is possible, a caution when only object damage is possible, and a warning when both levels are present.

Uppercase presentation in standard examples is illustrative, not an STE formatting requirement. Obtain capitalization, color, symbol, and layout rules from the governing safety or publication specification.

### Rule 7.2 — Start with prevention or a prerequisite condition

Open the safety instruction with a direct, accurate command that prevents the event. If a condition must be known first, put that condition before the command.

### Rule 7.3 — State the hazard or possible outcome

Explain the specific risk or result that can occur if the instruction is not obeyed. Prefer concrete consequences to abstract statements of importance. Do not invent a consequence; query missing hazard information.

## Section 8: Punctuation and Word Count

### Rule 8.1 — Do not use semicolons

All normal English punctuation marks can be used except the semicolon. Replace a semicolon with separate sentences. STE does not otherwise provide a full general-punctuation system; apply an official language or house reference.

### Rule 8.2 — Hyphenate directly related elements

Use a hyphen where it makes the relationship between words clear, including:

- Multi-word modifiers before a noun
- Spelled compound numbers and fractions
- A capital letter or numeral combined with a shape or configuration noun
- Specified compound technical verbs whose first element is a noun or another part of speech
- A prefix ending in a vowel before a root beginning with a vowel

Do not confuse a hyphen with a dash. Preserve authorized spellings and do not use a hyphen solely to manipulate word count.

### Rule 8.3 — Limit parentheses to recognized functions

Parentheses can contain:

1. A reference to an illustration or text
2. A letter or number identifying an item
3. A procedure step identifier
4. An abbreviation
5. Singular and plural forms shown together
6. An explanation of a word or part of a sentence
7. An alternative

Do not use parentheses as a general device for hiding unrelated or overlong information.

### Rule 8.4 — Treat a list-introducing colon as a sentence boundary

In a vertical list, count the lead-in before the colon as one sentence and each item after it as a new sentence. The lead-in and each item independently obey the 20-word procedural or 25-word descriptive limit.

### Rule 8.5 — Count parenthetical text in two layers

Count the whole parenthetical group as one word in its host sentence. Also count the words inside an explanatory parenthetical group separately as their own sentence under the applicable limit. A parenthesized identifier or abbreviation counts as one word in the host sentence.

### Rule 8.6 — Count protected multi-element expressions as one word

Each of these counts as one word:

- A number
- A number together with its unit of measurement, whether the unit is a symbol or word
- An abbreviation, acronym, or initialism
- An alphanumeric identifier
- Immutable quoted text, including a formula treated as quoted text
- A title, heading, placard text, or label text
- A proper name of a person, group, organization, or geopolitical entity

Do not count a number used only to identify a paragraph or work step. Do not treat ordinary changeable prose as quoted text merely to reduce the count.

### Rule 8.7 — Count a hyphenated unit as one word

A correctly hyphenated group counts as one word. The counting rule does not authorize incorrect or excessive hyphenation; first satisfy Rules 2.2 and 8.2.

## Section 9: Writing Practices

### Rule 9.1 — Reconstruct when direct replacement is insufficient

Use a different sentence construction when the dictionary alternative has a different part of speech, creates bad grammar, changes the meaning, is not suitable in context, or when the source word is absent from the dictionary.

Determine the intended meaning and action before revising. You may need to change word class, divide a sentence, delete genuine redundancy, revise a table heading, or request missing technical information. Recheck the complete passage after any reconstruction.

### Rule 9.2 — Apply every restriction on an approved word

Verify the dictionary definition, part of speech, form, context, and help note each time. Do not import a standard-English meaning. A small number of spellings have multiple approved entries; use only the entry that matches the sentence.

### Rule 9.3 — Do not create unapproved phrasal verbs

Two individually approved words do not become approved when combined into an idiomatic verb. Replace an unapproved phrasal verb with a direct approved verb. Only the small set explicitly listed in the dictionary is allowed, and only with its restricted meaning.

### Rule 9.4 — Keep terminology and repeated wording consistent

Use the same approved term for the same item and the same sentence pattern for the same recurring action when context and meaning are identical. Check procedures across nonadjacent steps, and check descriptions as a connected unit.

## General Recommendations

The GR items are recommendations, not mandatory STE rules. Label them accordingly.

### GR-1 — Use “that” to mark a subordinate clause

After constructions such as *make sure*, *show*, and *recommend*, include *that* when it makes the clause boundary clearer and supports translation.

### GR-2 — Review every use of “with” for ambiguity

The preposition can express association, accompaniment, or means. Rewrite when the reader could attach the phrase to different nouns or actions. Keep the primary action as the main verb.

### GR-3 — Give every pronoun one clear antecedent

Use only pronouns approved in the dictionary. If a pronoun could refer to more than one noun, repeat the applicable noun.

### GR-4 — Make the referent of “this” explicit

Do not let *this* ambiguously refer to an object, state, action, or prior sentence. Repeat enough context to identify the intended referent and causal condition.

### GR-5 — Check false friends

Confirm that a word resembling one in the writer's first language has the intended English meaning. Do not preserve a cognate merely because it appears familiar.

### GR-6 — Prefer English words to Latin abbreviations

Replace forms such as *e.g.*, *i.e.*, and *etc.* with clear English or omit them when unnecessary.

### GR-7 — Use inclusive, gender-neutral language

Avoid gender-specific pronouns and biased labels. A gendered biological term can be necessary in an applicable context such as medical documentation; otherwise use a neutral role or construction. Consult the governing inclusion guidance for matters beyond STE.

### GR-8 — Use possessives only when clear

The apostrophe-*s* possessive is permitted, but it can be difficult for international readers. Use it correctly; reconstruct with an *of* phrase or other clear form when uncertain. Apply external grammar and publication guidance.

## Cross-rule Review Traps

- A word replacement can fix Rule 1.1 while violating meaning (1.3), part of speech (1.2), direct action (3.7), sentence length (5.1 or 6.3), or grammar (9.1).
- A technical term absent from the dictionary is not automatically an error; test Rules 1.5–1.13 and the authorized termbase.
- A 20-word instruction can still violate one-instruction-per-sentence, imperative, condition-order, or safety rules.
- Passive-looking *be* plus participle can be a permitted condition adjective; ask whether it expresses a state or an action by an agent.
- A parenthetical group counts as one word in the host sentence but can create its own overlength sentence.
- A list can satisfy the count while failing grammatical connection, punctuation, content-class separation, or safety repetition.
- Uppercase warnings in examples are not evidence that STE mandates uppercase formatting.
- A checker result is evidence for review, never proof of compliance.
